1. Introduction

The popularity of new energy vehicles has facilitated the widespread
adoption  of  lithium-ion  batteries  (LIBs),  but  has  also  introduced  new
challenges,  such  as  range  anxiety  [1,2],  fire  and  even  explosion,  and
other safety hazards [3,4]. The solid electrolyte in ASSLIBs can effec-
tively address the potential safety issues associated with organic elec-
trolyte  and  impede  the  growth  of  surface  dendrite  on  lithium  metal
negative electrode. It has been recognized as an ideal solution to address
the energy density limitations and safety concerns of traditional LIBs [5,
6]. Solid-state electrolytes can be categorized into three types based on
their  composition:  inorganic  solid  electrolytes,  solid  polymer  electro-
lytes (SPEs) and organic-inorganic CSEs [7]. CSEs retain the excellent
flexibility  and  processability  of  SPEs,  as  well  as  the  remarkable  me-
chanical  strength  and  thermal  stability  provided  by  inorganic  fillers.
Furthermore,  compared  to  SPEs,  CSEs  exhibit  enhanced  overall  elec-
trochemical  performance,  including  improved  ionic  conductivity  and

electrochemical stability windows [8].

Inorganic fillers can be categorized as either inactive fillers, such as
TiO2  [9],  Al2O3  [10],  SiO2  [11],  or  active  fillers,  including  Li1.3Al0.3-
Ti1.7(PO4)3  (LATP)  [12–14],  Li1.5Al0.5Ge1.5(PO4)3  (LAGP)  [15,16],
Li7La3Zr2O12  (LLZO)  [17],  and  Li10GeP2S12(LGPS)  [18].  From  the
perspective  of  ionic  conductivity,  active  fillers  perform  better  than
inactive ones [19], due to the presence of three potential ion transport
channels in CSEs containing active fillers: the polymer phase, the pol-
ymer/ceramic  interface  and  the  active  ceramic  phase  [20].  More
importantly,  the  polymer/ceramic  interface  and  the  active  ceramic
phase exhibit higher ion transport rates [21,22]. Therefore, the conti-
nuity  of  the  ceramic/polymer  interface  is  crucial,  and  the  fibrosis  of
active ceramic electrolyte is considered the most effective approach.

Electrospinning is an effective method for preparing ceramic  elec-
trolyte nanofibers, however, it is limited by the long preparation period,
low efficiency and high stability requirements of precursor solutions for
NASICON solid electrolytes. In contrast, the thermal drawing of glass

Rare earth oxides, such as Y2O3, are network modifying oxides. High
field  strength  allows  them  to  accumulate  the  glass  network,  thereby
enhancing the structural integrity and thermal/chemical stability [27,
28]. Y3+
ions possess high field strength, which can enhance the ther-
mal/chemical stability. However, their large ionic radius prevents them
from entering the LGP lattice, thereby limiting their ability to expand
ion transport channels [29,30]. The formation of an intermediate phase,
however,  can  enhance  grain  connectivity  and  promote  grain  growth,
thereby reducing grain boundary resistance [30,31]. B2O3 is not only a
network forming oxide but also a low-temperature flux. When a small
amount of B2O3  (B2O3/P2O5  < 1) is added to the phosphate glass, the

LAGP grains, enhancing grain connectivity and reducing grain boundary
resistance [33,34]. Additionally, B2O3 flux not only reduces the melting
temperature  and  viscosity  of  glass  but  also  promotes  the  fluidity  and
formability of glass [35,36]. SiO2  is another type of network forming
