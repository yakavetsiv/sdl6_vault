Abstract

AC

With  the  commercialization  of  nanomaterials,  environmental  exposure  to  nanoparticles  (NPs)

has  raised  great  concerns  due  to  the  long-term  effects  to  human  body,  particularly  to  pregnant

women. Previous studies  found  that NPs  had an adverse  impact on placenta  in  mice, but care  must

be taken when extrapolating the results to human pregnancy in consideration of the great difference

between species. Here, we proposed a  microengineered 3D placental barrier-on-a-chip  microdevice

and  further  explored  complicated  placental  responses  to  NPs  exposure  in  vitro.  The  microdevice

recreated  near-physiological  3D  microenvironment  and  dynamic  conditions  in  fetal  maternal

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

circulation combined  with the extracellular  matrix and  flow. With  the exposure  to titanium dioxide

nanoparticles (TiO2-NPs), a common nanomaterial, a series of placental responses were investigated,

including  oxidative stress, cell apoptosis, barrier permeability, and  maternal  immune cell behavior.

By contrast to oxidative stress  and cell apoptosis,  placental barrier  integrity and  maternal  immune

cells  were  greatly  influenced  even  with  low  concentrated  NPs,  suggesting  the  potential  damages

triggered by NPs in our daily  life. Collectively, this  in vitro experimental  model of  human placenta

provides  a  simple  platform  to  study  environmental  exposure  to  NPs,  and  might  be  potential  for  a

wide  range  of applications  in  biological  study,  disease  treatment  and drug  assessment.

1.  INTRODUCTION

CEPTED MAN

As  a  rapidly  emerging  field,  nanotechnology  has  shown  great  potentials  in  a  wide  range  of

applications  in  ground  water, air,  soils,  industrial products, drug delivery, and disease therapeutics.

Environmental  exposure  to  nanomaterials  is  inevitable  because  of  their  widespread  use  in  our

everyday  life(Kessler 2011).  Therefore, a  high concern with  the effects of NPs to  human body  has

been  raised,  especially  the  nanomaterials  with  a  size  between  1  and  100  nm(Valsami- Jones  and

Lynch 2015). NPs are absorbed  into our body primarily through  the dermal, ocular, respiratory and

gastrointestinal  systems,  and  their  ability  of  accumulation  greatly  depends  on  the  size,  shape  and

surface  property(Miller  et  al.  2017).  However,  the  impacts  on  the  biological  systems  with  the

increased environmental exposure to these nanomaterials are not yet fully calibrated and understood,

especially  to the pregnant  women. For  instance, NP-based drug carriers  hold  great promise  for  the

development  of  targeted  therapies  to  specifically  treat  the  mother,  but  knowledge  on  NP-placenta

interactions  is  limited and concepts  how to steer placental translocation are  mostly  lacking,  mainly

due to the inherent variances in the development, architecture and function of placenta in tradit ional

models(Malysheva et al. 2015). Thus, there  is a great  need to develop  new  environmental exposure

models  to assess the risks  of nanoscale  materials  in  the  environment.

Organ-on-a-chip  technology,  as  a  newly  emerging  interdisciplinary  field,  presents  a  powerful

tool  for  the construction of complex in vitro experimental systems on  microchips that  recapitulate

the  microarchitecture  and  functions  of  living  human  organs  in  physiological  and  pathological

different  fields  and  disciplines,  organ-on-a-chip  technology  could  construct  progressively  more

complex  tissue  and  organ  models,  resembling  key  characteristics  involved  in  the  morphology,

function, and disease progression. Actually, a series of organ-on-a-chips have been developed in the

last decades, resembling the kidney, lung, liver, brain, gut in human body(Benam et al. 2016; Huh et

al. 2013; Huh et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2016; Li et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2017a; Zhu et

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1 Reconstitution  of 3D placental  barrier  on a chip

Placental barrier is a semipermeable membrane that acts as the interface between maternal and fetal

blood  circulation  during  gestation  (Figure  1a-b).  In  vivo,  it  is  composed  of  trophoblasts  and

endothelial  cells  separated  by  basement  membrane  matrix.  Herein,  we  designed  and  fabricated  a

CEPTED MAN

microdevice  for  the  construction  of  in  vivo-like  3D  placental  barrier.  Specifically,  the  chip  was

patterned  with  two  parallel  cell  channels  separated  by  the  middle  matrix  channel  (Figure  1c).  To

establish  the  multi- layered  structure  of  placental  barrier,  two  key  placental  cells  including

trophoblasts (BeWo) and endothelial cells (HUVEC) were seeded onto the opposite sides of cellular

matrix, respectively. The extracellular  matrix  in the  middle channel acts as a near-physiological 3D

scaffold  for  the  growth  of  placental  cells.  Perfusion  system  was  applied  to  two  cell  channels  and

created  near-physiological  dynamic  microenvironment  in  fetal  maternal  circulation  (Figure  1d).

AC

With the  flow, placental cells  finally  grew to  form  continuous cell  layer on the side elevation of the

matrix  (Figure  2a).

As  in  many  epithelia,  trophoblasts  form  microvilli  on  the  maternal  surface  to  increase  surface

area,  thereby  offering  sufficient  nutrients  to  the  developing  fetus.  There  are  studies  demonstrated

that  fluid  shear  induced  microvilli  formation  in  placental  trophoblastic  cells(Miura  et  al.  2015).

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

Thus, as a key feature of placental barrier, the formation of microvilli in BeWo cells were examined

in our microsystem. As shown in the enlarged image, we could observe dense microvilli covered the

trophoblastic  cell  layer  after  culture  under  dynamic  conditions  (Figure  2a).

GLUT1  is  an  essential  glucose  transporter  in  human  placenta  which  is  tightly  involved  in  the

transmission  of  glucose  across  placental  barrier.  GLUT1  is  examined  to  locate  on  both  the

microvillous  (maternal  surface)  and  basal  membranes  (fetal  surface)  of  the  trophoblast  layer,  but

with  three-fold  higher  expression  in  the  microvillous  side(Lager  and  Powell  2012).  In  order  to

check  the  function of  microvilli,  the expression of  GLUT1 was  identified through the performance

of  immunostaining  (Figure  2b  and  Supplementary  Figure  S1).  GLUT1  was  highly  expressed

around  the  trophoblast  barrier  with  dynamic  flow,  and  the  cross-sectional  view  displayed  the

In  this  study,  we  developed  an  engineered  3D  placental-barrier-on-a-chip  microdevice  for  the

investigation  of  complicated  placental  responses  to  environmental  nanomaterials.  Through  the

addition of extracellular  matrix,  in vivo-like 3D  microenvironment  was created on  the chip, which

might  facilitate  the  morphology  and  function  of  placental  barrier.  Furthermore,  the  flow  in  both

trophoblast and endothelial cell channels resembled the dynamic environment  in  maternal and  fetal

circulation in the body. With the exposure to NPs, placental responses were analyzed including ROS

production, cell apoptosis, the integrity and permeability of the barrier, as well as maternal immune

cells.  Although no dramatic alterations  in  ROS production and ratio of dead cells  were observed  in

the  case  of  low  concentrated  NPs,  dysfunctions  of  placental  barrier  and  impaired  immune  cell

behavior  was  identified,  which  implied  the  potential  damages  induced  by  TiO2-NPs.  All  data

reflected  the  interlocking  placental  barrier  system  in  response  to  NPs  exposure,  and  displayed  a

the  incorporation  of  stem  cells  and  biomedical  engineering,  thereby  greatly  facilitating  our

understanding  of human  reproductive  health  and disease.
