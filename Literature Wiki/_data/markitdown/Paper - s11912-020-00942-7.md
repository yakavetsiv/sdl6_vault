An Update on Surgical Margins in the Head Neck Squamous Cell
Carcinoma: Assessment, Clinical Outcome, and Future Directions

Arjun Singh 1 & Burhanuddin Qayyumi 1 & Pankaj Chaturvedi 1

Published online: 30 June 2020

Abstract
Purpose of Review Failure to achieve tumor-free margins is the single largest cause of death for head neck cancer patients. At the
same time, it is the only factor that is in complete control of the surgeon. This review summarizes evidence for the definition,
clinical implications, and methods to achieve optimal margins.
Recent Findings The previous universally followed definition of adequate margin (5 mm in final histopathology) has been
disputed. Various biological, optical, and imaging adjuncts can aid in achieving optimal margins. Extent of resection and margins
in human papilloma virus (HPV)-positive oropharyngeal cancers and following induction chemotherapy remain controversial.
Though practiced widely, frozen section–guided margin revision has not conclusively shown improved local control rates. The
role of molecular assessment of margins is promising but not established.
Summary The definition of adequate margin differs according to the site in the head neck region. Currently, the 5-mm margin at
final histopathology is the most commonly accepted definition of an “adequate” margin.

Keywords Margins . Adequate margins . Head neck cancer . Squamous cell carcinoma . Frozen section

Introduction

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common cancer
occurring in the head and neck region [1]. Each site has a
specific management approach that may include surgery, ra-
diotherapy, systemic therapy, or a combination of these mo-
dalities [2]. If surgery is the preferred option, the locoregional
control (LRC) is best achieved by complete removal of tumor
[2]. A cuff of disease-free tissue excised surrounding the tu-
mor, called the margin, often measures this “completeness.”
When tumor is found at or close to the resection margin, local
control (LC) and survival outcomes are adversely affected [3].

What Is an Adequate Margin and Why Is It
Important?

Failure to achieve tumor-free margins is the single largest
cause of death for HNSCC patients [10]. At the same time,
it is the only factor that is in complete control of the surgeon
[11]. There still exists a controversy on the definition of the
free margin. In a large survey among the American Head
Neck Society members, the most common response for a clear
margin was greater than 5 mm. Most respondents considered
carcinoma in situ as a positive margin while 76% considered
dysplasia at the margin as negative [12]. On the other hand,
the College of American Pathologists guidelines recommend
that moderate and severe dysplasia at resection margins
should be considered as positive margins [13]. This highlights
the lack of uniform criterion defining margin adequacy.

Fig. 1 Definitions of margins

Margins are either positive, i.e., tumor “cut through,” close,
or negative with varying definitions of distances from the
tumor and edge of the specimen [11]. Once the mucosa is
incised, regardless of the cutting instrument used, the speci-
men margins tend to be lesser than those measured before
resection [14]. This difference exists due to the intrinsic tissue
properties causing shrinkage at the margins [14]. Greater
shrinkage is seen in tongue cancers than the buccal mucosa
[15, 16]. Considering shrinkage of approximately 30% that
takes place, a resection with greater than 8-mm clinical margin
is recommended to obtain a 5-mm microscopic margin.

Pattern of invasion (POI) at the tumor-host interface is an
important prognosticator that needs consideration when deal-
ing with margins. The POI at the deep margin has been found
to strongly influence LC rates even in early stage oral and
glottic cancers [17–19]. Infiltrative rather than cohesive POI
is also known to increase the risk of locoregional recurrence
(hazard ratio (HR) 1.46) and more than double the risk of
dying of cancer (HR 2.34) [20].

A systematic review has concluded that in vocal cord sur-
gery, a close margin could be considered to be less than or
equal to 1 mm; in the larynx, less than or equal to 5 mm; in the
oral cavity, less than or equal to 4 mm; and in the oropharynx,
less than or equal to 5 mm [4]. When interpreting the results,
one should be cognizant of the fact that these aberrations in
margin distances have been derived only from retrospective

data. No prospective trials have been undertaken to determine
the optimal margin status for HNSCC. Surgeons have always
aimed to achieve the conventional 10 mm in vivo margin and/
or a 5-mm pathological margin [2, 3, 11]. However, due to
various reasons, a lesser margin distance than that anticipated
is reported, which is used in their post facto analysis.
Therefore, if a surgeon aims to achieve a 2-mm margin a
priori, due to tissue shrinkage and processing, the reported
margin will be considerably less than anticipated. It is also
important to acknowledge the difficulty in obtaining measure-
ments from retrospective archival specimens in which prior
frozen sections may have altered the distances. Since less than
5-mm margin distances were considered close or positive, the
adjuvant therapy received in these patients may also be a con-
founding factor in outcome analysis.

Oral and Oropahryngeal Cancers

A study has found that each millimeter increase in clear sur-
gical margin decreases the risk of death at 5 years by 8% in
oral cancers [21]. Patients with positive margins had a 2.5-fold
increase in risk of death, and those with close (less than 3 mm)
margins had a 1.5-fold increase in risk of death when com-
pared to patients with margins greater than 3 mm [21].
Another study found a local recurrence rate of 28% for mar-
gins less than 1 mm and 17% for 1 mm margins [22(cid:129)]. For
margins less than 2 mm, 3 mm, 4 mm, and 5 mm, the differ-
ences were less varied ranging from 11 to 14%. In this series,
all patients had frozen sections taken from the surgical defect

underestimating the true distance of the surgical margins and
potential inaccuracies of surgical defect sampling [22(cid:129)]. A
recent study focusing on oral tongue cancers challenged the
5-mm margin consensus to redefine close margins [23(cid:129)]. The
optimal cutoff associated with good local recurrence-free sur-

than 5 mm (HR 1.31). All other comparisons were significant-
ly different (HR positive margin 9.03; HR 0.01 to 2.2 mm
margin 2.83) [23(cid:129)].

Interestingly, not all studies demonstrate better outcomes
for adequate margins compared to inadequate margins [17,
24–26]. Why would these reports of “inadequate” resections
be associated with better outcomes than most other “inade-
quate” resections? An estimated 25% of negative margins
and 75% of the positive margins go on to develop a local
recurrence [8]. One research group has attempted to classify
positive margins to include four histologic findings at the
margin: (1) margin closeness (tumor within 5 mm), (2) pre-
malignant change, (3) in situ cancer, and (4) invasive micro-
scopic cancer [27]. When compared to negative margins, they
found that their definition of positive margins had worse LC
rates, but their classification of positive margins were all at an

equally high risk for local failure and increased mortality [27].
Another group divided the margin into zones rather than an
absolute free and involved status based on the surveillance
technique used. This ranged from visible, palpable, histologic,
immuno-histologic, and molecular zone [28].

For resections involving the maxilla or mandible, an ade-
quate margin has traditionally been considered as 5–10 mm
from involved or suspicious bone, or removing two teeth on
either side of involved edge [29]. One of the only studies
commenting on the adequacy of bone margins in advanced
oral cancers recommends taking the bony cut at least 15 mm
away from the clinically discernible tumor, especially in cases
where the bony component is greater than the soft tissue com-
ponent of the tumor [30(cid:129)].

Impact of Human Papilloma Virus Status on Margins
in Oropharngeal Cancer

Since the human papilloma virus (HPV)–associated oropha-
ryngeal carcinomas (HPV+OPC) have a better treatment re-
sponse and survival outcome than HPV-negative oropharyn-
geal carcinoma (HPV−OPC), recent studies have assessed the
impact of margin status on disease control rates in them [31].
The large surgical trials for HPV+OPC have considered a
close margin as anything less than or equal to 3–5 mm and a
positive margin when tumor was present less than 1 mm or at
the ink surface [32, 33]. They used this histologic margin
criterion to prescribe appropriate adjuvant therapy and assess
outcomes. It is important to note their methodology mentions
that the surgeon should aim to achieve a minimum of 10 mm
gross visual margin, so as to arrive at their defined adequate
margin definition in final histology [32, 33]. Interestingly,
studies have found that inadequate margins (even positive)
do not seem to confer as significant a prognostic impact in
HPV+OPC (HR for positive margins in HPV+OPC 1.61 vs
2.11 in HPV−OPC) [34, 35]. Another study found that pa-
tients with p16-positive oropharyngeal cancer and positive
margins after excision had a low risk of local recurrence de-
spite most receiving radiotherapy alone as adjuvant treatment
[36].

Impact of Neodjuvant Chemotherapy on Margins

Neodjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) in HNSCC has been ex-
plored for a long time with the primary aim of reducing sur-
gical margins and distant metastasis rates [37]. The controver-
sy lies as to whether pre- or post-induction chemotherapy
response margins should be considered [38]. A match-pair
analysis of 215 oral cancers that received NACT has shown
a zero margin positivity rate and 3.3% close margin rate, when
margins were taken as per the tumor response [39]. Another
study of 721 borderline operable oral cancers has shown an
8.5% inadequate margin rate when considering post-response

margins [40]. The only two randomized trails that assessed the
role of NACT in oral cancers considered margins of at least
10–15 mm from the initial clinical evaluation or from the
marks that were placed before induction chemotherapy [41,
42]. They also stated that the final choice was left to the judg-
ment of the responsible surgeon. Taking this margin criterion,
both these studies have shown locoregional relapse rates rang-
ing from 25 to 31% [43]. The pattern in which induction
chemotherapy sterilizes the periphery of the tumor is still un-
certain. Most studies also prescribe concurrent chemoradio-
therapy to all patients that have received NACT, confounding
the impact of margin status on local outcomes [39, 40]. No
consensus exists on the appropriate margin distance after
NACT.

Larynx and Hypopharynx Cancers

Contrary to the definition of adequate margins for oral
and oropharyngeal cancers, an adequate margin for glottic
cancers is usually limited to 1 to 2 mm [5, 6]. This holds
true especially in early stages where the impact of inade-
quate margins has not been found to significantly impact
LC and overall survival (OS) rates [44–46]. This could be
due to inherently good outcomes of these cancers.
Additional surgical defect biopsies could help predict lo-
cal recurrences and a wait-and-see policy could be
adopted in patients with positive specimen margins and
negative surgical defect biopsies [47]. In advanced laryn-
geal cancers, a significant association with “cut-through”
deep margins and decreased LC is present, regardless of
whether adjuvant therapy has been administered [48]. As
part of the Department of Veterans’ Affairs Laryngeal
Cancer Study, a study reported 50% survival outcomes
for clear margins, 57% for close (less than 5 mm) mar-
gins, and 27% for involved margins [49]. Patients with
close and positive margins received significantly higher
doses of postoperative radiation that could have mini-
mized the survival differences between these groups.
Importantly, the data suggests that high-dose postopera-
tive radiation does not eliminate the benefit of obtaining
wide clear surgical margins [49]. In the hypopharyngeal
tumors, submucosal patterns of spread could predispose to
inadequate margins [50]. A study has shown that the ex-
tent of submucosal tumor extension was least in the supe-
rior direction (3–10 mm) and highest in the medial (2–
37 mm), lateral (2–37 mm), and inferior direction (3–
35 mm) [50]. The same group has also emphasized on
adequate radial clearance and suggested that a radial
clearance of less than 1 mm is an independent prognostic
factor for OS, DFS, and regional recurrence-free survival
[51]. Hence, greater margins up to 30 mm have been
propagated in hypopharyngeal tumors.

Cutaneous SCC

Cutaneous SCC has a more universally acceptable criterion
for defining margins. The adequate margin recommendation
stands as 4–6-mm peripheral margins for well-defined low-
risk lesions [52]. High-risk status is dependent on the tumor
behavior and size at a particular site and the margin should be
greater than 6–10 mm [52]. The deep margin status is not
mentioned in all guidelines but is more anatomical barrier
based; i.e., it should be at the level of hypodermis, sparing
the aponeuroses, perichondrium, and periosteum if they are
unaffected by tumor extension [52].

What Is the Role of Frozen Section–Guided
Intraoperative Margins Assessment?

In the simplest sense, intraoperative assessment of tumor-free
margin is dependent on clinical appearance and palpation of
the tumor [2, 3]. Frozen section (FS)–guided margin assess-
ment is a popular tool as it provides a rapid microscopic ex-
amination of the resected specimen [53]. Studies including a
recent publication have reported concordance rates of FS and
histopathology upwards of 97%. The most common drawback
with FS is the false negative rate with a majority being due to
sampling errors [54]. A survey among American Head Neck
surgeons suggests a wide variation on the margin assessment
techniques for FS, where 67% only sampled the surgical de-
fect to determine the intraoperative margin status [12]. This
technique has been shown to have a high rate of inter- and
intra-observer variability in identifying the “true new margin”
[55]. A large majority of the survey respondents also consid-
ered the FS-based revised margins as negative margins [12].
Interestingly, a recent meta-analysis has concluded that mar-
gin revision of initially positive margins to “clear,” based on
FS guidance, does not equate to initially negative margins
[56(cid:129)(cid:129)]. They found that this revision does not significantly
improve LC. In fact, margins that require repeated FS for
microscopic clearance carry a higher risk of recurrence ex-
ceeding 25% [57].

As a cost-effective alternative, a study has compared the
efficacy of FS-guided resection and gross examination (GE) to
achieve adequate margins. They found that both had similar
rates of detecting inadequate margins (6.63% in FS vs 6.69%
in GE). They also found no survival benefit when FS was used
for margin assessment [58]. The same group reported that the
chance of an inadequate margin was very low if the gross
surgical margin was 7 mm or more [59]. Additionally, they
found no difference in the precision of FS vis-à-vis GE with a
7-mm cutoff [59]. Other studies have shown a 0.7–5% benefit
of FS in head neck cancer patients due to immediate margin
revision and avoiding unnecessary adjuvant treatment [60,
61(cid:129)]. These studies also found similar cost-benefit ratio

been performed in contrasting economic scenarios [60, 61(cid:129)].
FS-guided revision of margins is still not cost effective and

Bone Margins

Bone margins are often not amenable to intraoperative tissue
analysis due to its high mineral content and need for decalci-
fication [62]. Hence, bony margins have traditionally been
determined preoperatively based on imaging and clinical
judgment. Although not practiced in many centers, few tech-
niques have been reported in the literature to address this
issue. A study has sampled thin slices of the proximal 1 cm
of mandibular cancellous bone that were subject to standard
FS techniques. They were able to predict the final pathologic
margins in 97% of cases [63]. Other techniques with variable
efficacies include cytologic assessment of marrow scrapings,
trephining 1 or more 5 mm cores of bone from the resected
margins and cryo-freezing them, intraoperative microwave
processing, and elastic scattering spectroscopy [64–68].
Regardless of the technique used to process bone, intraopera-
tive interpretation of the bony margins remains unreliable.

How to Improve the Accuracy of Achieving
Adequate Margins?

Due to a greater understanding of the impact of margins on
outcomes, there has been a surge in development of various
adjuncts for assessment (Fig. 2).

assessment

Fluorescent Visualization

Although staining methods have fallen out of practice for pre-
resection margin assessment, major advances have been made
in biological and optical imaging. Using ultraviolet or visible
light radiation of suitable wavelengths, photons in the tissue
can be activated resulting in intrinsic fluorescence, i.e., auto-
fluorescence [69]. A crucial step forward has been the intro-
duction of near-infrared (NIR) imaging. Since the absorption
coefficient of normal tissue is at a minimum when using NIR
light, an imaging window (650 to 900 nm) is available that
allows for minimal light scattering and increased tissue pene-
tration [70]. Tumor tissue can also be labeled by certain
fluorophores that target the various hallmarks of cancer such
as increased growth signaling receptors, limitless replicative
potential, sustained angiogenesis, increased proteolytic activ-
ity, tissue invasion, and metastasis [70, 71]. Studies have com-
pared conventional and fluorescence visualization (FV)–guid-
ed surgery in oral SCC and premalignant lesions. They found
better 3-year survival rates in the FV-guided group for the oral
SCC (40.6% vs 6.5%) and pre-invasive lesion (39.3% vs
8.1%) [72].

Optical and Imaging Techniques

With the advancement in spectroscopy techniques, surgeons
now practice “real time” visualization of cancer cells at the
apparently healthy margins [72]. One such device is the
Raman spectroscope that is a sensitive vibrational spectro-
scopic method that can detect subtle biochemical changes
during the onset of disease. It can distinguish SCC from sur-
rounding tissue with a high sensitivity and specificity (99%
and 92%, respectively) [73]. In recent studies, these images

have been linked to histopathological evaluation of same sec-
tions, generating pseudo-color Raman images for the easy of
in vivo margin assessment [74].

Another automated device, the MasSpec Pen, rapidly iden-
tifies molecular profiles of tissues using a small volume of
surface droplet [75]. The device distinguishes normal from
tumor cell by characterizing protein, lipids, and other metab-
olites. Currently in the pre-clinical phase, this device has a
high sensitivity (96.4%), specificity (96.2%), and overall ac-
curacy (96.3%) to detect in vivo cancer cells [75].

Contact endoscopy has been used to accurately distin-
guish healthy mucosa, dysplasia, and invasive carcinoma
during surgery [76, 77]. It has a specific advantage over
FS in glottis tumors; in that, it is a non-invasive technique
to map lesions in real time before resection. Studies have
reported a 100% correlation with FS, sensitivity of 80%,
specificity of 100%, and an accuracy of 93% for diagnos-
ing malignancies [76, 77].

High-resolution microendoscopy has also gained popular-
ity with a study showing a mean accuracy, sensitivity, speci-
ficity, negative predictive value, and positive predictive value
of 95.1%, 96%, 95%, 98%, and 91%, respectively, to distin-
guish benign and malignant mucosa in real time [78]. Phase
I/II trials are underway to determine the accuracy of reflec-
tance confocal microscopy in the detection of tumor at mar-
gins. With the help of automated video mosaicking, a larger
field of view is now possible for intraoral imaging [79].

Narrow band imaging (NBI) uses a high-resolution endo-
scope and two distinct wavelengths of light (415 nm blue and
540 nm green) that limits penetrance to the mucosal surface
for improved visualization of vessels within [80]. Recent stud-
ies have demonstrated its applicability in early glottis cancers
where local recurrences developed in around 24% cases treat-
ed with white light alone and 2–3.6% in the NBI groups. The
2-year recurrence-free survival was 82% in the white light
alone group and 98% in the NBI group [80, 81].

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is an emerging tech-
nology that uses light to provide high-resolution cross-sec-
tional images on a micron scale in real time [82]. Due to the
limitation in depth that can be imaged (mean epithelial thick-
ness of 360 mm and 567 mm at free and involved margins,
respectively), it is only useful for assessing peripheral mucosal
margins [82].

Routine ultrasound has also been used to aid in margin
assessment, especially in the tongue cancers [83]. A study
has shown an increased mean adequacy of deep margin being
achieved in ultrasound-guided resections (9.8 ± 5.2 mm for
guided vs 4.0 ± 2.03 mm without sonography-guided resec-
tion in T stage-matched tumors) [83].

Simply adopting a greater understanding of the anatomy
and tumor extent has an impact positive margin rates. Since
preoperative assessment of advanced digital imaging became
a routine for oral and oropharyngeal cancers, an interesting

study among experienced surgeons reported a significant re-
duction in positive margin rates from 28 to 5% [84].

Combinations of the above techniques have also been used
for margin surveillance with higher rates of accuracy than that
individually reported [85]. Whereas the efficacy of all these
technologies appears positive, the logistics of their use as a
routine technique remains to be proven. Unfortunately, most
techniques only examine the mucosal surface. Surgeons are
more likely to encounter an inadequate deep tissue rather than
mucosal margin. In a review of 301 oral and oropharyngeal
cancers, deep soft tissue margins were involved in 87% of
resections with inadequate margins, while inadequate mucosal
margins alone were rare [86].

Enbloc and Multibloc Resection

Much of the resection done for HNSCC is enbloc with the
exception of certain laryngeal, oropharyngeal, and sinonasal
SCC [87]. After the resection is complete, the surgeon must
orient and document the unstretched margins before sending it
to the pathologist. For intraoperative margin assessment, sam-
pling done from the resected specimen has been found to be
more accurate than biopsies obtained from the surgical defect.
A study demonstrated that surgeons are likely to be off the
intended revision areas by more than 10 mm in a third of the
cases when the margins are obtained from the surgical defect
[55]. This could be due the defect disorientation caused by
retraction of the surrounding tissues. The same authors have
suggested marking the various margin points within the defect
with clips or sutures, before sending the resected specimen for
intraoperative assessment [55]. A randomized trial has an-
swered this debate by demonstrating a 29% improved nega-
tive margin rate when the margins were sampled from the
specimen [88]. However, it is important to note that in glottis
tumors, surgical defect assessment should be avoided because
the extra tissue taken can unnecessarily harm subsequent vo-
cal function, especially when inadequate margins have been
shown to have no significant impact on outcomes [44–46, 89,
90].

With a paradigm shift towards conservative surgical ap-
proaches, multibloc resections for HNSCC are mostly restrict-
ed to transoral laser microsurgery (TLM), robotic surgery, and
functional endoscopic sinus surgeries (FESS) [91]. TLM has
the advantage of three-dimensionally mapping the deep mar-
gins under high magnification. FESS can adequately debulk
tumors to achieve good visualization for achieving an ade-
quate margin around the stalk [91]. Certain anatomical con-
straints in the upper aerodigestive tract, like in lateral pharyn-
geal wall tumors, often make wide margins unobtainable.
Using transoral surgery and multibloc margin mapping, a pro-
spective study has shown low positive margin rates and good
local control. They considered a margin to be adequate unless

Molecular Assessment

Compliance with Ethical Standards

The biologic behavior of individual cancers impacts the ability
to achieve local control. Various grading systems have been
devised to classify the aggressiveness and pattern of invasion
of HNSCC [93, 94]. But even after achieving an adequate
three-dimensional margin around the tumor on histopatholo-
gy, the possibility of failure remains as high as 25% [8]. The
presence of field cancerization should be taken into account
when dealing with margin assessment [95]. The current
methods have a limited sensitivity to conclusively classify
margins as adequate. This has triggered the development of
novel molecular and genetic techniques of margin assessment
[96].

There is enough evidence to show the altered expression of
pertinent biomarkers at the margins such as tumor suppression
genes (p53 and TP53), oncogenes (epidermal growth factor
receptor), proto-oncogenes (Her-2), loss of heterozygosity
(LOH), eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E),
and promoters of hypermethylation [9(cid:129), 96]. These changes,
not identified on histopathology, could explain the initiation
of premalignant and malignant changes resulting in recurrence
and second primary tumors [97]. However, the routine use of
molecular assessment of surgical margins has significant lim-
itations primarily as it is expensive, labor intensive, cannot be
used in vivo and can only reveal genetic alterations in the
mucosa and not the deep soft tissue margin [96].

Conclusion

Inadequate surgical margins adversely impact disease-related
outcomes, subjecting patients to avoidable and unnecessary
adjuvant therapy. Currently, 5-mm margin at final histopa-
thology is the most commonly accepted definition of an “ad-
equate” margin. Though there is emerging literature regarding
the safety of lesser margin distances in HPV+OPCs, it still
remains an area of active research. Extent of resection and
margins following induction chemotherapy remains contro-
versial. However, learning from previous organ preservation
trials, margins can be obtained according to the post-response
extent of the tumor. Though practiced widely, FS-guided mar-
gin revision has not been conclusively shown to improve LC.
