Fluorescence-Guided Surgery for Assessing Margins
in Head and Neck Cancer
A Review

Shravan Gowrishankar, MA, MD; Stan Van Keulen, MD, DDS, PhD; Aviva Mattingly, MD; Hidenori Tanaka, MD, PhD;
Mike Topf, MD; Kyle Mannion, MD; Alexander Langerman, MD; Sarah Rohde, MD; Robert Sinard, MD;
Jennifer Choe, MD; Baran Sumer, MD; Mark Varvares, MD; Eben L. Rosenthal, MD

IMPORTANCE Head and neck squamous cell cancer (HNSCC) is associated with a higher
positive margin rate compared with most other cancers in surgical oncology. This rate has
remained stagnant over the past 3 decades. Fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) is a promising
tool to address high positive margin rates in multiple solid tumor types, including HNSCC. This
review summarizes data from a decade of FGS research in this tumor type to outline how
fluorescence can help improve margin clearance, especially at the deep margin. The
principles presented can be broadly applied using HNSCC as a model disease.

OBSERVATIONS In contrast to the superficial mucosal margin, the deep margin is especially
challenging for surgeons to visualize and contributes to the vast majority of positive margins
and subsequent sequelae in HNSCC. Using fluorescence in vivo can highlight residual disease
in the resection bed, while using fluorescence ex vivo can highlight how close the tumor
margin is from the resection surface of the specimen, guiding sampling and further resection.

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE Currently, there are several clinical trials investigating FGS
to improve margin clearance in HNSCC, with many fluorescent agents already approved for
use in other cancer types. As additional agents are brought into clinical use, it will be critical
to understand how this technique will or will not improve oncological management in our
patients. To address this important point, we present 2 key areas where surgeons will
consider use of these FGS: to assess the peripheral mucosal margin and the deep surface
of the tumor. We outline how in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence can be used for this purpose.
We summarize data from multiple sources to explain how FGS is most likely to help with deep
margin clearance.

W ith approximately 50 different trials registered in

ClinicalTrials.gov,1-4 fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS)
has the potential to improve high positive margin rates
in surgical oncology. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
(HNSCC), positive margins range from 15% to 30%5-7 and consti-
tute a significant challenge in the field as they can increase local
recurrence and mortality risk by up to 90%.8,9 Notably, positive
margin rates in HNSCC have not significantly changed over the past
30 years, highlighting the need for new technology to improve
margin clearance.10

When considering tumor margins, they can be classified into
(1) the superficial/mucosal margin and (2) the deep margin. The mu-
cosal margin refers to an area of grossly uninvolved, healthy epithe-
lium surrounding the tumor’s exposed surface, whereas the deep
margin refers to 4 to 5 mm of healthy tissue beyond its most inva-
sive points (Figure 1). The deep margin is the depth of normal tis-
sue between the tumor edge and the cut surface of the specimen.
As a result, it cannot be easily assessed because it requires the sur-
geon to continuously estimate the distance from the tumor with

mainly palpation as a guide. Thus, deep margins are more often at
risk for positive margins in HNSCC as opposed to missed superficial/
mucosal margins,6 which are mostly seen well under white light.

FGS is a promising tool to help obtain clear deep margins while
balancing the need to preserve healthy functional tissues of articu-
lation and swallowing. Here, we summarize data from multiple
sources to explain the application of FGS to benefit margin identi-
fication in HNSCC regardless of the agent used—assuming that it spe-
cifically identifies cancer (not the surrounding microenvironment)
and is bound to the tumor (does not leak from the tumor immedi-
ately after binding). Using HNSCC as an example, we consider these
principles to be broadly applicable to the use of FGS is a range of
tumor types.

accurately detect the mucosal margin.11 Autofluorescence is a phe-
nomenon where endogenous fluorophores in tissue (collagen, nico-
tinamide adenine dinucleotide plus hydrogen, porphyrins, etc) will
absorb light of a particular wavelength and subsequently re-emit
this light at a different wavelength. Neoplastic development leads
to structural changes, which reduce autofluorescence in cancer-
ous tissue; a change that can be visualized on the mucosal surface
intraoperatively. A large, multisite, randomized clinical trial com-
pared autofluorescence-guided surgery to white-light guided sur-
gery in more than 400 patients with stage T1-T2 oral squamous
cell cancer12 and found that using autofluorescence did not reduce
the rate of positive specimen margins, improve local recurrence
rates, or improve overall survival. This negative result from a well-
conducted study could be interpreted as a failure of the technol-
ogy: that autofluorescence is not able to distinguish tumor from nor-
mal mucosal surfaces.13 However, autofluorescence has been shown
to delineate cancer tissue at the mucosal surface with high sensi-
tivity and specificity in HNSCC.13 An alternative explanation is that
surgeons can easily distinguish the mucosal margin of HNSCC can-
cers, and therefore incremental improvement in technologies to
identify the mucosal margin might not significantly improve pa-
tient care.

After the circumferential mucosal margin is completed, blood
and tissue retraction constantly obscure the surgical view. This
confounds assessment of deeper margins where each millimeter of
distance will impact patient survival.14 In contrast to the mucosal
surface, autofluorescence cannot assess the deep margin because
the light does not penetrate beyond a millimeter. Perhaps more
limiting is intraoperative bleeding, surgical manipulation, and in-
flammation that interferes with the autofluorescence properties of
tissue, resulting in unreliable signals at deeper margins or the wound
bed after resection.

The Deep Margin

Systemically administered agents can just as easily be topically
applied to the epithelial surface or deep margin to detect regions of
exposed tumor. However, fluorescent agents investigated as topical
agents have not advanced beyond feasibility studies.15 Although the
US Food and Drug Administration approval process for these agents
is much simpler, they do not seem to be as effective as systemic agents.
In HNSCC at least, the specimen’s deep surface is often very heter-
ogenous, resulting in a complex surface with characteristics that
can confound topical application. In contrast, there are several sys-
temically injectable fluorescent agents that are used in oncological
surgery.16 Theseincludeindocyaninegreenandmethyleneblue.These
agents have been used to visualize breast and brain cancer but have
low tumor specificity and also leak from cancer cells, which limits their
use.17-19 Approved systemic agents include 5-aminolevulinic acid for
malignant gliomas and pafolacianine for lung or ovarian cancer.20-22
Intravesical administration of 5-aminolevulinic acid is approved for use
in bladder cancer, but as a tool for detection, rather than defining
surgical margins.23 Pegulicianine was recently approved for detec-
tion of residual tumor within the surgical breast cavity.24 However, no
systemically administered agents have been specifically approved for
head and neck cancer. In this tumor type, a range of fluorescent agents
are being developed to differentiate normal from cancer tissue. One
target is the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), which is over-
expressed in HNSCC.25 These include anti-EGFR antibodies such as
panitumumab or cetuximab conjugated with fluorescent dyes (eg,
IRDye800)3,4,8,11,12,26 or alternatively antibody fragments that have
a shorter half-life and are thought to reduce background.27 Other
imaging agents in clinical trials include pH-activated agents that are
conjugated with fluorescent indocyanine green (eg, ONM-100 and
pegsitacianine) and fluorescent-labeled small molecule agents
and peptides (eg, PARPi-Fl).28-30

Fluorescence to Reduce Sampling Error
at the Deep Margin

The current standard for detecting residual tumor at the deep mar-
gin after resection is palpation and white light visualization of the
wound bed and/or specimen after removal, followed by frozen sec-
tion analysis (FSA) of suspicious areas. However, the sensitivity of
these 2 methods (inspection and FSA) is low, and there remains
significant variability in techniques including using tumor-bed mar-
gins or specimen-based margins, often determined by surgeon
preference.31,32 We suggest that effective use of FGS has the po-
tential to localize the region where the specimen is most likely to be
positive under appropriate conditions. This can be used to guide the
location of frozen section sampling, both in vivo during surgery and
on the excised specimen ex vivo, both of which can be useful in re-
ducing sampling error at the deep margin.33,34

Most positive margins after oral cavity resections occur on the deep
surface. For example, in a study of more than 300 patients, 23% of
resections had a positive margin, with a positive deep margin oc-
curring in more than 85% of this subgroup.6 Furthermore, a deep
margin was the only positive margin in more than 70% of these re-
sections with positive margins. This is the problem for which fluo-
rescence-based surgical imaging agents can improve our clinical
evaluation.4

Preoperative administration of fluorescent agents can detect
cancer relative to adjacent healthy tissue in vivo.36-38 Under opti-
mal conditions, these targeted, systemically infused agents have rou-
tinely been shown to differentiate cancerous and healthy tissue
with high sensitivity and specificity.31,36 Optimal conditions in-
clude dimmed or switched-off overhead lights to reduce interfer-
ence with tumor fluorescence.

With respect to the deep margin, in vivo fluorescence can de-
tect residual disease in the wound bed with high sensitivity.33 This
can be used to guide sampling, although the size of residual tumor
that can be detected is often greater than 1 to 2 mm depending on
the agent.33 Effective use of FSA relies on a surgeon’s ability to
predict which area is the closest margin or tumor-involved areas via
inspection and palpation. However, the sensitivity for identifying
positive margins using this approach is low (36%).31,32 In this set-
ting, fluorescence-based mapping has the potential to improve mar-
gin sampling by highlighting areas of residual disease in the deep
resection bed, reducing the odds of sampling error.33 Efficient use
of FSA analysis is critical because processing time is usually 20 to 30
minutes per frozen section.39 Using fluorescence as an additive tool
to the aid might allow the surgical team to screen the wound bed
or specimen during resection for more precise sampling.

Despite the highlighted benefits to margin sampling using fluo-
rescence imaging, there are some limitations while using this tech-
nique in vivo. These challenges include interference from ambient
(eg, surgical overhead) light within the operating room, shadowing
and scattering within the oral and wound cavity, and ergonomic prob-
lems arising from the absence of custom-designed imaging de-

vices to fit in narrow areas (eg, the oral cavity).40 These issues that
disrupt surgical workflow, such as switching off overhead lights, will
likely be overcome with appropriate instrumentation and light fil-
ters, which have not been widely adopted yet. Newer imaging de-
vices also function well with dimmed overhead lights that do not
need to be switched off.35 However, these issues can be mitigated
using ex vivo pathological tumor imaging as an adjunct to in vivo
imaging.

Pathologic Imaging
Pathologic or ex vivo imaging occurs when the tumor specimen is
resected and imaged within the operating room. This can provide
similar information to in vivo imaging but is far more precise be-
cause imaging happens within a controlled environment, yielding
higher tumor-to-background ratios.31 Similar to in vivo imaging, fluo-
rescence can be used to guide frozen section sampling on the speci-
men. Ex vivo imaging devices have several distinct advantages over
in vivo hardware; the specimen is placed in a device that seals out
ambient light, and fluorescence intensity can be measured. While
in vivo imaging primarily provides qualitative data because of vari-
ance in ambient light from the operating room, ex vivo imaging is
more precise because of a controlled closed-field environment,
which can allow correlation between fluorescent intensity and mar-
gin distance. In fact, fluorescent agents detect tumor tissue even
when it is underneath 4 to 5 mm of normal tissue using near infra-
red wavelengths.31 In this setting, the fluorescence intensity in-
creases the closer the tumor tissue approaches the cut surface of
the tumor specimen.41 These data can be used to create a fluores-

Conclusions

As FGS is introduced to the clinic, it will be necessary to under-
stand where it can improve detection of cancer in the resection
bed and reduce sampling error of deep margins in head and neck
cancer. Collating a decade of FGS research in head and neck can-

cer, this article outlines how in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence
imaging can be used to help achieve this goal. Mucosal margins are
mostly well identified using traditional white light and tactile cues.
In contrast, missed deep margins contribute to the majority of
positive margins after resection. Intraoperative fluorescent “map-
ping” of excised tumors with ex vivo imaging can provide data on
how close tumor margins are to the deep resection surface. Intra-
operative in vivo fluorescence imaging can reveal areas of residual
disease in the tumor bed. In combination, the information pro-
vided by in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging can guide fur-
ther examination and sampling to help achieve fuller resection of
the deep margin.
