Guidelines on Unattended/Autonomous Experiments in the Laboratory

Scope
Unattended or autonomous experiments are experiments where the apparatus for the experiment has been left
to run while the people responsible for the operation of the experiment are not in the area to monitor events
and no one has been assigned to cover the absence of the main experimenter. In some cases, there may be
remote monitoring or logging in process, however this is still considered unattended or autonomous. In some
labs there may be robotic equipment that is conducting experiments, either in a contained area like a stationary
robot, or in some cases with free moving robotics that can independently work with equipment such as fume
hoods in the open lab.

This document provides a framework for risk assessment and does not replace local risk assessment.

By the end of this document, you should be able to:

•  Understand what responsibilities are for; the Chair, the Principal Investigator, students/staff and

Environmental Health and Safety (EHS).

•  Understand what safety considerations must be looked at when embarking on experiments that will be
unattended. This will include criteria for inclusion/exclusion of experiments with respect to being run
unattended.

•  Understand what types of experiments may need to be considered for further mitigation of risk when

being run unattended.

•  Understand what notification needs to be posted on the exterior lab door.
•  Understand what the minimum requirements for an unattended operations standard operating

procedure (SOP) would be.

•  Understand how to assess what other requirements may need to be fulfilled.

Definitions

•  Unattended: Experiments running for extended periods while no lab member is assigned oversight of the

experiment.

•  Automated: Tasks executed by machines, where each cycle is started by a human.
•  Autonomous: Workflows initiated and executed entirely by the system without human intervention.
•  Release: An event, including but not limited to, a water leak, spill of hazardous liquid, release of vapour,

fire or explosion, etc.

Responsibilities
Department Chair/equivalent or their Designate is responsible for:

•

Informing the PI of any interruptions in service of which they are aware that may interrupt unattended
experiments.

•  Having a process as applicable for working with PI’s to implement this Guideline.

Principal Investigators (PIs)/Designate are responsible for:

•  Providing any required approval for unattended or autonomous experiments and equipment.
•  Reviewing and assessing potential releases or incidents that could lead to harm or property damage.
•  Advising the department of unattended operations that are occurring in their lab.
•  Consulting with the department and seeking approval from the chair as applicable for high or

medium risk activities (see sections 5 and 6) where the consequences of a failure cannot be kept
within the containment zone.

•  Ensuring that any activities are as safe as is reasonably achievable in an experimental environment.
•  Ensuring that an emergency contact is available during the duration of the experiment.
•  Ensuring that any needed signage is posted so that staff and students are properly informed of any risks

presented by the activities.

Students/Staff conducting Unattended/Autonomous Activities are responsible for:

•  Consulting with the PI on any planned unattended or autonomous experiments.
•  Following any instructions from the PI on the planned experiments.
•  Following all applicable SOPs, manufacturers recommendations and industry standards as applicable.
•  Following these guidelines.

Environmental Health and Safety (EHS):

•  Shall update this standard periodically as needed.
•  Assist Departments/Institutes with local risk assessment when needed; Incorporate industry standards

and best practices to update this guideline.

Considerations
Unattended Operations:

•  Are discouraged except where necessary and with approval of the PI (Principal Investigator).
•  Should be a well understood process.
•  Should not be conducted unattended if there is a high chance of adverse outcome or a high hazard in

the event of a release and/or incident

•  Must be approved by the PI or designate who knows the work and the implications of a system failure

best.

•  May be routine and low risk. These operations can be covered by a lab SOP.

•  May have considerations and precautions that are unique.
•  Must be assessed for risk and mitigation – please use the Risk Assessment Tool as a guideline and note

that some experiments may have issues not specifically covered in the tool.

•  Must have secondary containment sufficient to hold the full projected volume of the experiment

without overflowing. For instance, a tray to contain any liquids that may escape.

•  Must have at least a sign posted in the lab to inform others in the lab that an autonomous or

unattended experiment is in progress and the hazards presented by the operation. The sign must be
near the site of operations and should be large enough that basic information can be read from a safe
distance. See sample sign in appendix.

•  Some operations may also require a sign on the door alerting after hours personnel such as caretakers,
maintenance personnel and Campus Security that there are unattended operations going on inside and
with brief instructions on what to do if ventilation fails or an alarm sounds. An after-hours phone
number for a knowledgeable lab person must be available to Campus Security, although it does not
need to be on the sign if the number has been supplied to Campus Security in advance. See sample sign
in appendix.

•  Have an emergency shutoff wired in for any autonomously moving equipment.
•  Keep the area around the experiment clear of hazardous or combustible (fuel) materials – no waste

containers, reagents, solvents, boxes etc.

•  Consult the Guideline on University Built Equipment for any equipment that is designed or built by

the experimenters.

At a minimum include the following checks as part of your SOP before beginning:

•  Equipment is in good condition and operating within normal parameters – check logbook as needed.
•  Any water connection.
•  The volume remaining in any inert gas supply
•  Available space for effluent in any collection or overflow containers
•  Condition of hoses or pipe connections.
•  Condition of electrical connections.
•  Area is clear of any items that could burn or spread a fire.
•  Containment is adequate for the volume of the experiment.
•  The area is clean and uncluttered.
•  Any combustible or self-igniting materials are localized and away from heat/spark sources.

Risk Assessment Tool for Chemicals
The consequence of any release of materials is influenced by their volatility (the tendency of the materials to
become airborne), the flammability, the reactivity, and the toxicity. The NFPA fire diamonds found on SDSs can
be used to give a quick assessment of the risk presented by any release.
For any given material that might be released, rate the severity of the release by taking the NFPA 704 (fire
diamond) numbers for flammability, reactivity, and health from the diamond on the SDS or label and sum them.

(Image courtesy Marcos Rodríguez Bobadilla, via Wikimedia Commons)

If the sum of the numbers is 6 or more consider the material to be HIGH RISK.
If the sum of the numbers is 4-5 consider the material to be MEDIUM RISK.
If the sum of the numbers is 3 or lower consider the material to be LOW RISK.

Risk Assessment for Biologicals
For any given material that might be released, risk is based on the containment level assigned for the material.
CL2 and above is High Risk, CL1 is low risk.
