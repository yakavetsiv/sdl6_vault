Makerspace and Prototyping Safety Guideline

Health and Safety Considerations for the Management of Makerspaces, including Prototyping and
Product-Development Spaces

Makerspaces, including any prototyping and product-development spaces, are facilities that
encourage collaboration, hands-on learning, experimentation, and creation, including do-it-
yourself projects using hand and power tools. The types of activities and equipment may overlap
with other facilities, such as machine shops where activities are carried out by professional or
technical staff; however, unlike those settings, the activities in makerspaces tend to be self-guided
by the user.

The types of activities, equipment, machinery, and tools at each makerspace facility will vary
depending on the objectives of the space. The purpose of this document is to articulate some high-
level considerations for departments that are planning for, or are currently operating, such a space
and should not be interpreted as an exhaustive or comprehensive list. Maker and prototyping
spaces need to prioritize safety for all users, both internal to the university (employees, students)
and external (visitors, community members, collaborators) as applicable.

While fabrication device use, including 3D printers, is common for research and academic
purposes, it is important for departments to understand that there are certain activities – such as
replicating patented objects, mass-producing items for commercial sale, or printing weapons or
medical devices – that can give rise to signiﬁcant legal and safety issues.

It is expected that departments will review this document and carefully consider the manner in
which their makerspaces are being used by the University community. Departments should contact
EHS (ehs.office@utoronto.ca) if they have any concerns or require assistance.

For all equipment that is built or signiﬁcantly repurposed, modiﬁed, or altered for use in a research
lab where equipment design or modiﬁcations have been directed by U of T staff or students,
whether or not the equipment is built at U of T, please refer to the Guidelines on University
Designed/Built Equipment Safety.

For any questions regarding core facilities, deﬁned as any research facilities across the institution’s
three campuses offering specialized research services or equipment to the U of T community,
please visit the VPRI Institutional Core Facilities webpage.

Design Considerations

Layout and Space Usage

•  Adequate space for movement, machinery, and materials-handling (ergonomics).
•  Storage areas are designed to provide adequate space for personal protective equipment

(PPE), tools, equipment, and materials (including hazardous chemicals, storage of
compatible chemicals).

•  Waste management should include proper sorting, storing and organizing in including off-

cuts and shavings. When applicable, a designated area for hazardous, ﬂammable,
explosive and toxic waste should be allocated.

•  Workstations for prolonged use are located away from printers, including 3D printers, and

other emissions.

•  Signage and labels are posted with thorough and applicable safety considerations. Any
integration of any new equipment into the building envelope requires consultation with
the property manager of the building (e.g. anchoring storage shelves to the wall, installing
a window AC unit, connecting power tools to power, etc.).

Engineering Controls

•  Adequate room ventilation based on activities, exhaust (including local exhaust

ventilation), and ﬁltration when applicable (e.g., chemicals, welding fumes, wood dust,
heat generated from equipment, etc.).
Include fume hoods or other local ventilation in the design of the makerspace when
dealing with volatile or hazardous substances to reduce exposure to chemicals.
•  Adequate lighting, including task lighting over work areas and benches for small parts

assembly.

•  Electrical needs shall be adequate for the Electrical Safety Association (ESA) certiﬁed

•

equipment and follow the EHS Electrical Equipment Guidance.
Incorporate noise controls such as enclosures, baffles, purchasing quieter equipment,
etc. to reduce noise from equipment, activities, space infrastructure, etc.
Include dust controls when items are drilled, cut, sanded or polished and fume controls
for soldering, welding, etc.

•  Select furniture with ergonomic features such as adjustability (worksurface), lumbar

support, armrests, footrests, and tilt angles (chairs). Design for ﬂexibility, using furniture
that can be easily moved and reconﬁgured to suit changing makerspace needs.  Look for
furniture that can be easily adjusted to ﬁt the height, weight, and posture of different
users. Use ﬁnishes that can withstand spills and repeated cleaning, e.g., avoid absorbent
or ﬂammable materials.

•

Incorporate ﬁre protection measures and equipment (at a minimum, smoke and ﬁre
detection and alarm systems), including extinguishers appropriate for the hazards
•  Provide clearly deﬁned emergency egress routes. Floor markings are used, where

applicable, to demarcate between hazard or work zones and walkways.

In rare circumstances where outside items are permitted for specialized activities,
additional safety measures will be needed. Please contact EHS for additional
recommendations and assistance.

4.  In most cases, it is expected that these spaces operate during normal business hours of the
building. This is to ensure staff is available to monitor activities and to provide assistance,
especially in case of an emergencies. In rare situations where spaces are open during non-
business, additional procedures are required (e.g. rotating on-call schedule for staff,
limiting activities to low risk ones, arranging for Campus Safety or patrols to check on the
space hourly, check-in and check-out procedures, etc.). Please contact EHS for assistance.

5.  Develop rules and a process for reviewing and approving projects to avoid inappropriate
objects from being made (e.g., weapons, weapon parts, medical devices, items that can
cause physical or emotional harm, etc.).

3D Printers

•  Only approved or comparable materials (manufacturer recommended) should be used in

the 3D printer.

•  Ensure appropriate ventilation applicable to the emissions produced (e.g. ultraﬁne

particles).

•  Consider using feedstock that produces less toxic emissions (e.g. ABS, when compared to
PLA, requires higher temperatures and results in more toxic byproducts such as hydrogen
cyanide).

•  Consider the power of moving heads and the requirements for safeguarding (e.g. some 3D

printers with a powerful moving head will push objects (hand) out of the way).
•  Consider how scaffolding will be removed and waste disposal considerations.
•  Develop procedures for cleaning 3D printers, for removing items safely form the printers
and procedures to follow in case of failed print (e.g. burns from hot nozzle and extrusion
heads, cuts from tools, shards or sharp edges, tie bag hair or baggy clothing when exposed

Chemical Safety

•

If work involves Controlled Products under WHMIS, take WHMIS training. For information on
EHS courses, please refer to EHS Training matrices for Facilities & Admin staff and Lab
Personnel.

•  Ensure appropriate spill kits are available, and staff are trained in their use. For large spills,

contact Environmental Protection Services (416-978-7000).

•  Develop procedures for the storage of chemicals (e.g. approved ﬂammable liquid cabinet,

appropriate separation of incompatible materials).

•  Ensure chemical Safety Data Sheets (SDSs) are in HECHMET. (Resources: Chemical

Storage Flowchart and Chemical Storage Table. )

•  Where available, eyewash is ﬂushed weekly (logged) by the makerspace and emergency
showers are tested annually by Facilities & Services. If a chemical fume hood is used, an
annual certiﬁcation by EHS is required (refer to the sticker on the sash or contact
ehs.office@utoronto.ca).

Prohibited Items

At minimum, the following items are prohibited:

•  Weapons and weapon parts
•  Guns and gun parts

It is illegal to manufacture or possess any ﬁrearm  — including a 3D-printed ﬁrearm or certain
components of a ﬁrearm — without the appropriate license issued by a Chief Firearms Officer. For
more information, Please refer to: https://www.canada.ca/en/public-safety-
canada/news/2024/08/new-ﬁrearms-licensing-requirements-to-increase-public-safety-coming-
into-force.html.

Medical Devices

responsibility of all facilities users to thoroughly review all applicable regulations, legislation,
guidance or directive and comply with them accordingly. Some resources have been included in the
