Abstract
The past decade has witnessed remarkable advancements in autonomous systems, such as automobiles that are evolving from traditional vehicles to ones capable of navigating complex environments without human intervention. Similarly, the rise of self-driving laboratories (SDLs), which leverage robotics and artificial intelligence to accelerate discovery, is driving a paradigm shift in scientific research. As SDLs evolve to expand the scope of chemical processes that can be performed, it is essential to bring safety to the forefront to ensure that the necessary safeguards are in place to mitigate against potential accidents that range from near-misses to catastrophic failures. This Perspective examines the development trajectory of SDLs, juxtaposing their development with those of other autonomous technologies, with a particular focus on safety. We explore current safety status and concerns, identify opportunities for innovation to shape this rapidly evolving landscape, and reflect on the actions the SDL community can take moving forward.

Current safety state of self-driving laboratories The trajectory of self-driving laboratories
The concept of laboratory automation has existed for decades. The term ‘self-driving laboratory’ was first introduced by Maruyama in 2018 (ref. 37). However, the roots of laboratory automation can be traced back to the 1940s, when the rise of industrial automation began influencing the field of chemistry. Notably, Bosch and Mittasch developed robotic systems for catalyst discovery in the Haber–Bosch process38. In 1966, Merrifield et al.39 showcased an automated platform for peptide synthesis using flow systems and batch reactors, all controlled by a mechanical computer. By the 1980s, Zymark advanced laboratory robotics by creating robotic arms to automate tasks such as sample preparation, which revolutionized chemical laboratory workflows40,41. With the rise of computational power and AI, the concept of using machine learning to build closed-loop robotic systems in chemistry laboratories emerged. In 2004, King et al. introduced the idea of a ‘robot scientist’ that could determine gene functions by executing ML-driven experiments, interpreting results and providing feedback for future experiments42. To support high-throughput experimentation, the team developed a fully automated robotic system named ‘Adam’43, which identified 12 new gene functions by generating hypotheses and conducting thousands of experiments over several days without human intervention. Over the past 5 years, rapid advancements in robotics and AI, including large language models (LLMs) and computer vision, have significantly accelerated the development of SDLs by enhancing existing capabilities. These works span robotic platforms and software systems, each contributing to faster, more autonomous scientific discovery. Introduction
Self-driving technologies have emerged as transformative innovations in various domains, from autonomous vehicles (AVs) to smart home systems. These advancements reflect a broader trend towards automation, wherein machines are increasingly capable of performing tasks that were once the exclusive domain of humans. Perhaps the most advanced self-driving technology to date, AVs have evolved from futuristic concepts into near-commercial realities over the past decade1, using technologies such as sensors, cameras, radar, LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) and machine learning (ML) algorithms2–5 to navigate and make real-time driving decisions. For example, although requiring driver oversight, Tesla’s Autopilot and Full Self-Driving systems have made notable strides towards autonomy6,7. Similarly, companies such as Waymo have developed a driverless Robotaxi that has achieved as high as 85% lower crash rates compared to human drivers8, illustrating the potential safety benefits of removing human error from the driving equation. These advancements mark a step forward in transportation technology and provide a blueprint for broader autonomous systems. Despite the potential benefits, safety concerns remain a critical barrier to the widespread adoption of AVs as a mainstream transportation option9. High-profile incidents and performance studies have underscored the difficulties of managing various systems in AVs, such as accurate detection of pedestrians10 and obstacles11, robustness against malicious attacks on sensors12, and effective interaction with other human drivers13. Although some situations are edge cases that occur infrequently, they can all cause substantial harm if mismanaged. Beyond technical challenges, public trust14, cybersecurity threats15 and regulatory issues16 further complicate the integration of AVs into everyday life. In parallel to the development of AVs, and in a scientific discovery context, self-driving laboratories (SDLs) are set to revolutionize how science is done. SDLs use robotics, automation, artificial intelligence (AI) and ML algorithms to autonomously conduct experiments, gather and analyse data, and iteratively refine decision-making processes in research with minimal human intervention. Although automation enhances safety by limiting human exposure to hazardous chemicals, increasing laboratory autonomy introduces new safety considerations17. Research SDLs often operate in open experimental spaces and, thus, require adaptive safety architectures capable of both preventing dangerous scenarios and responding to unforeseen risks. As SDLs advance, ensuring their safety and reliability is paramount to fostering their widespread adoption as a transformative technology. Existing laboratory safety standards and advancements18–26 can serve as valuable references when adapting safety protocols for SDLs, particularly in addressing new risks introduced by automation and AI. Traditional laboratory safety typically follows a widely recommended hierarchy of controls, prioritizing more effective measures such as hazard elimination and substitution, followed by engineering and administrative controls, and lastly the use of personal protective equipment (PPE)27. This framework provides a useful lens for thinking about laboratory safety governance as laboratories transition from human-centric workflows to singular automation, and ultimately to collaborative and autonomous SDLs. In routine laboratory automation used to replace certain repetitive single-step tasks, such as autosamplers and robotic liquid handlers, engineering controls have a central role, including hardware interlocks, physical shielding and fail-stop mechanisms. In these cases, well-established industrial safety practices would provide important guidance and benchmarks for implementation28–31.

Current safety status, concerns and challenges
As we expand the capability of the research community to develop sophisticated SDLs that can manage increasingly challenging chemistries, it is essential to bring safety to the forefront of SDL research to ensure necessary safeguards against potential accidents, from near-misses to catastrophic failures. In this section, we will examine existing software and hardware infrastructure in SDLs to assess the presence of embedded safety features that facilitate safe research practices (Fig. 1), highlighting parallels with industries that harness similar automation technologies.

A well-designed orchestration platform is crucial for constructing efficient, high-throughput experimental workflows and ensuring robustness and safety55,56. First, user access control and authentication are essential to regulate who can initiate or modify experiments, ensuring that only authorized personnel with proper training can execute high-risk protocols. Second, proper software management to maintain and enforce stable versions of control software for instruments is recommended to avoid issues caused by software failures, especially when coordinating across multiple parallel instruments in high-throughput set-ups. Third, real-time monitoring and alerts should be in place to continuously record and interpret the state of the laboratory, keeping operators informed about ongoing activities within the SDL systems63. This real-time data can be sourced from a variety of systems, such as torque and proximity sensors that come pre-built on commercial robotic arms, standalone detectors for toxic gases such as carbon monoxide, or, if needed, bespoke systems in cases wherein a commercial instrument does not contain methods to report a crucial parameter, owing to either hardware limitations or closed-source software. The data collected across these devices can then be pooled and monitored by the orchestration software to display which experiments are running, the state of various devices, and any failures that require human intervention64. In addition, automatic error handling is another necessary feature to maintain safety and minimize disruptions. Automatic error handling should provide methods for intermediate data retrieval and the ability to restart protocols from the point of error. It should also ensure appropriate system responses to avoid catastrophic behaviour, such as shutting down solution pumps when pressure fluctuations are
detected to prevent explosions56, quenching exothermic reactions if temperature exceeds a predefined safe range to prevent fires, or alerting personnel to avoid entering the laboratory space if toxic gases are detected. These safe ranges can be determined by human experts with in-depth knowledge of the chemicals and reactions involved, or be autonomously guided by ML safety models that were trained on diverse safety data, which will be discussed further in the section ‘Cognitive safety’. In more severe cases, integrating an option to automatically contact emergency services such as campus security, ambulance services or the fire department could further enhance safety by ensuring an immediate response. The system could also generate detailed summaries about currently ongoing experiments, potential hazards and recommended actions, providing emergency responders with crucial information to assess the situation swiftly and act accordingly. Most crucially, in analogy with software tools focused on safety, the orchestration platform should verify the experiment pipeline before executing new procedures. This involves checking for both protocol integrity and compliance with chemical safety guidelines for the planned chemicals and reactions. Protocol integrity can be maintained through code checks that validate parameter values and conditions before execution. Alignment with standard operating procedures and chemical safety guidelines can be checked with laboratory simulations or by performing dummy experiments using safe substitutes (for example, replacing liquid chemicals with water) to provide confidence before running actual high-risk experiments65. Overall, the system should adhere to predefined safety instructions and proceed with the task only after all safety checks are successfully completed. Experiment execution and operation. SDLs can maximize safety by using experimental techniques that avoid using hazardous chemicals in bulk. One enabling technology is the use of microfluidics and continuous-flow technologies74. Compared to conventional batch techniques, microfluidics and continuous-flow reactors have faster
heat, mass and momentum transfer owing to their compact dimensions and high surface-to-volume ratios while also requiring smaller reagent volumes and shorter reaction times. This enables greater control over reactions, allowing potentially hazardous processes to be conducted safely with minimal risk processes that could otherwise be prohibited for safety reasons (for example, reactions involving toxic or explosive intermediates, high-pressure conditions, or temperatures above the boiling point of a solvent). For example, lithiations, which typically require cryogenic temperatures of −78 °C in batch conditions, can be safely conducted at 25 °C in flow systems while eliminating cryogenic risks75,76. A comprehensive review by Gutmann et al. further highlights how continuous-flow conditions have enabled the safe manufacturing of organic intermediates and active pharmaceutical ingredients, which typically involve synthetic steps with hazardous reactions or extreme process windows77,78. For certain reactions of interest, continuous-flow technology could, thus, serve as an ideal experimental platform within SDLs, enabling safer exploration across a broader range of reaction conditions. High-throughput experimentation, which can conduct reactions at the micromolar to nanomolar scale, also shares the advantages of microfluidics in avoiding handling bulk chemicals and having compact reactor dimensions. In addition, the highly standardized nature of high-throughput experimentation wellplates enables safety practices developed by one group to be quickly adopted by other practitioners running a similar workflow. Robotic systems, such as mobile robots and robotic arms, can significantly enhance safety in SDLs, as they reduce direct human contact with hazardous substances. However, skilled personnel are still required to monitor experiments, address unexpected malfunctions, and intervene in emergencies. A wealth of safety knowledge and protocols can be drawn from industries that have exploited robotics long before the advent of modern SDLs79. Safety standards for robotics in the industry begin with basic principles when working with hazardous electrical equipment, such as installing guards that prevent limbs or hair from being caught in dangerous apparatus and ensuring that built-in lockout mechanisms are in place to de-energize the system80. In addition, there are standards specifically designed for robotic devices that allow human researchers to work safely within the same physical space81,82. Contemporary SDLs have performed several successful implementations. For example, the mobile robotic platform developed by Burger et al.49 uses laser scanning to prevent collisions. If an obstacle is detected within a bounding box, the robot will slow down and stop completely unless the obstacle is removed. The attached robot arm remains tucked in when the base is in motion and, during operation, runs a constant collision protocol that triggers a safety stop if a certain unexpected torque is detected. Another approach to enhancing safety, especially during contact and physical interaction, is incorporating compliance mechanisms into the robot83. These mechanisms allow the robot to adapt its movements when coming into contact with the human body or obstacles, thereby reducing the risk of injury or damage. Another recent notable example is ORGANA44, which relies on constrained motion planning to prevent spills during vial transfers and leverages the reasoning capabilities of an LLM to identify unexpected outcomes. When anomalies are identified, the system prompts users for ‘human-in-the-loop disambiguation and troubleshooting’, enabling collaborative decision-making. Moreover, ORGANA includes a report generation feature that summarizes observations and records key results and data analysis. This report generation feature can be readily extended to support safety by developing appropriate standard operating procedures and risk mitigation strategies based on the chemistry and SDS information of relevant chemicals, retrieving associated safety metrics and reported incidents involving safety violations, as well as documenting potential near-misses that may occur during operations. ORGANA uses an integrated approach to task and motion planning, specifically PDDLStream84, to plan how tasks are executed. It relies on the Planning Domain Description Language (PDDL), a structured, rule-based language for defining planning problems. One can incorporate additional safety-related rules into the PDDL description to ensure safety. If creating or updating a detailed planning domain becomes too complex, alternatives such as digital twin simulations85–87 or heuristic methods (for example, motion planning) can help generate and verify plans. Safety considerations can still be addressed through simulation-based verification, even if not explicitly defined in the planning language. A similar approach is adopted in RABIT (Robot Arm Bug Intervention Tool)88, developed by Wattoo et al. to tackle safety concerns in SDLs wherein diverse devices are expected to interact with one another in complex ways. The key framework of RABIT involves (1) a rule set that combines general-purpose rules applicable to most SDLs with custom rules tailored to specific set-ups, (2) monitoring of command sequences to intercept actions that fail to meet required preconditions, and (3) enforcement of these rules using simulation, a low-fidelity test bed, and a production environment. Nonetheless, it is crucial to recognize that although these safety features are essential for safe experiment execution and operation, they cannot completely eliminate accidents in practice89,90. Training and adherence to safe practices are extremely important when humans work in tandem with robots. Even the most advanced built-in safety features will have a limited effect if users lack experience working
with or alongside robots or are unfamiliar with safety protocols. For instance, when Malm et al. studied severe industrial robot accidents in Finland from 1989 to 2006, the leading causes of injuries were inadequate safeguarding and insufficient warnings when working with robots91. Another study by Yang et al. of occupational injuries in Guangdong province, China, has revealed that workplace injuries generally increased in the first 2 years of adopting robotics before levelling off and descending past the third year92. In addition, non-routine operations, such as robot troubleshooting, maintenance, repair, inspection and cleaning, also represent a particularly vulnerable area often overlooked in safety planning. For example, Malm et al. further showed that most accidents occur during robot troubleshooting91. Similarly, an analysis of machinery-related fatalities in Korean industries by Kim et al. between 2014 and 2018 has revealed that most accidents occurred during non-routine tasks93. SDL practitioners should learn from the experiences of these industries by prioritizing training and enforcement of safe working practices. Safety measures in SDLs should also address both routine and non-routine activities. By doing so, personnel can help prevent mistakes reported by others, protecting the health and environment of everyone involved.
Data safety, cybersecurity and dual-use. Data safety and cybersecurity are other important aspects for reliable operation of SDLs, safeguarding both the the physical safety of personnel and large volumes of information generated and stored on SDL servers34. Many SDL instruments are connected to the Internet for remote operation and cloud-based management94. Such connectivity exposes these systems to potential malicious attacks. For example, attacks against robots from leading industry manufacturers have been found simply by performing Internet scans and string queries95. Many manufacturers also freely provide firmware and online documentation. Although this enables convenient updates and troubleshooting for end users, it allows attackers to discover vulnerabilities by reverse engineering the system firmware95. The consequences of such cybersecurity breaches from insider and external attackers are many-fold. For example, tampered instruments, stolen files and manipulated experimental protocol would compromise the integrity of the results. In some cases, attackers can even overwrite built-in safety measures to sabotage scientific instruments and physically harm personnel working close to compromised equipment95,96. Although a rare occurrence, this type of risk should also be accounted for in both academic and industrial research settings97. These consequences underscore the need for discussion on the extent to which an SDL should be connected to the Internet. Although smaller-scale SDLs with fewer components can effectively operate offline, only occasionally transmitting data to the cloud, their larger counterparts using mobile robotics may require some Internet connectivity to orchestrate actions between their various components and monitor their operations remotely. Establishing any point of Internet connectivity in an SDL should involve robust cybersecurity measures and stringent monitoring to prevent system hijacks and unauthorized access. This can include data encryption, IP whitelisting, multi-factor authentication protocols, and storing sensitive research data on secure remote servers. Some examples of cybersecurity strategiesimplemented in existing SDLs include the A-Lab, which restricts Internet access to the control computer, while all instruments interface with it through a local area network45. Closing the safety gap
The call to close the safety gap in SDL design, implementation and operation requires a multifaceted approach. This section will explore how AI technologies can be leveraged to enhance chemical and operational safety in an SDL from a technological and scientific standpoint. On the social front, we will discuss the critical role of fostering a robust SDL safety ecosystem through personnel training, cultivating a culture that gives priority to safety, and enhancing collaboration among
different stakeholders (Fig. 2). Additionally, we will touch on some emerging safety challenges and highlight the importance of evolving SDL safety standards to match the rapid pace of their development and deployment as we advance SDLs towards conducting high-risk experiments.
The role of AI in improving safety
To improve chemical and operational safety in an SDL, AI technologies can be leveraged to replicate and enhance the essential skills of a human scientist, including cognitive decision-making, visual perception and precise handling113. This comprehensive framework is necessary to ensure robust, preventive and real-time risk assessment and management for these autonomous systems to operate securely and safely.
Cognitive safety. To achieve intelligent decision-making and risk assessment, SDLs must develop an ‘experimentalist intuition’ similar to human chemists, allowing them to avoid unintended safety risks114, minimize the suggestion and execution of potentially life-threatening actions, and dynamically adjust protocols. Specialized safety agents can facilitate this by dividing SDL safety management into distinct, focused responsibilities. Examples include chemical risk assessment agents that evaluate potential hazards and implement appropriate countermeasures, safety critic agents that identify and correct potential errors, biases or harmful recommendations in the outputs of the primary agent115–117, and accident response agents that evaluate and handle real-time incidents. Early iterations of such capabilities are already evident in platforms such as ORGANA44 and ChemCrow70, providing a solid foundation for further developing such specialized agents. A good example of a specialized safety AI agent is SciGuard, developed by He et al., which incorporates short-term and long-term memory on ethical codes, safety standards, regulatory frameworks, hazard databases and interaction histories to prevent misuse118. The integration of multiple specialized safety agents within SDLs can, thus, allow for more comprehensive and dynamic safety management. Our recent experience in the context of agent-driven SDLs shows that adding more specialized agents to a system improves its performance119. Beyond safety AI agents, risk-aware systems with flexible self-constraints can also enhance chemistry SDL safety by performing on-the-fly risk assessments. These systems must be able to operate with both known constraints (for example, volume restrictions based on reactor capacity and hazardous reagent combinations) and unknown constraints (for example, equipment failures, unstable molecules and unforeseen side reactions). Recent advances in experimental planning algorithms, such as Bayesian optimization, have shown great promise in handling these constraints145–150. Furthermore, safe reinforcement learning algorithms151–154, which aim to maximize rewards while satisfying safety constraints during the learning process, also demonstrate immense potential. Future research can focus on designing chemical safety-aware constraint functions for these algorithms, as well as developing uncertainty quantification methodologies to provide reliability assessments of algorithm outputs, especially for empirical, data-driven approaches such as Bayesian optimization155.
Vision-based safety. Visual perception is fundamental for precise robotic manipulation of materials156,157 and real-time safety monitoring158. In the context of object and material manipulation, algorithms developed by Xu et al. and Wang et al. allow robots to better perceive transparent objects51,159,160 (Fig. 3a), which is important to manipulate laboratory apparatus. The HeinSight models161,162 enable robots to monitor liquid levels, colours and turbidity and to detect liquid phases (Fig. 3b). Computer vision techniques are also used to estimate material properties, such as viscosity163. Beyond individual objects and materials, vision-based environment awareness and safety monitoring further ensures safe SDL operations. On the algorithmic side, 3D scene reconstruction methods164,165 can be used for robot obstacle avoidance166 and to construct robot workspaces in simulated environments167, which are valuable for training robust robot policies and improving task execution. At the same time, advanced imaging technologies such as hyperspectral
cameras can help to monitor temperature fluctuations168 and detect chemicals169,170, whereas computer vision techniques can be used to analyse visual data to identify hazards such as chemical spills, notice operational abnormalities such as set-up malfunctions and deviations from standard procedures, and detect human presence near robotic equipment to ensure PPE compliance and prevent accidents. To further enhance visual-based decision-making, incorporating uncertainty quantification into computer vision and perception models can help robots to detect both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties171,172. By leveraging Bayesian decision theory, these systems can act conservatively in unfamiliar environments, actively query for additional information, or request human feedback when needed, improving safety and decision reliability173. Future advancements would also probably see greater application of augmented reality technologies, along with the development of multi-modal perception foundation models174. Augmented reality-based systems can enhance the performance and safety of scientists by providing real-time instructions and monitoring their actions175, whereas multi-modal models can combine vision with other sensory inputs such as audio, vision, weight, pH, touch and humidity to enhance the precision and robustness of autonomous laboratory perception.
Robust chemical handling and motion planning. Enhanced visual cognition in autonomous systems should be complemented by improved robotic manipulation skills. Fundamental robotic manipulation skills required in an SDL setting can be categorized into three distinct classes. First, pick-and-place actions49,52,176 are essential for sample transfer, for example, by grasping vials, microplates, racks and beakers. Robots must carefully control movement to prevent collisions and sample spillage. This can be improved by implementing constraints on the orientation, velocity and acceleration of an end-effector, either conservatively52, or adjusted dynamically177 based on variables such as liquid volume and viscosity. Second, liquid and powder handling skills48,69,87, including pouring, dispensing, shaking and mixing, may also require motion constraints to prevent spillage and maintain sample integrity. Last, the manipulation of soft, rigid and articulated objects demands robust handling and continuous monitoring to verify the successful execution of each action178,179. To implement these skills, robots can rely on classical control methods and motion planning techniques that heavily rely on userdefined policies to execute predefined movements. AI-based strategies include reinforcement learning that require efficient simulators and reward engineering, as well as imitation learning wherein humans train the system through multiple demonstrations. Despite significant progress, achieving robust performance across varying workspace settings, lighting conditions and dynamic parameters remains challenging. Developing intuitive and natural interfaces, such as teleoperation systems180 or hand-held manipulation interfaces181, can facilitate direct skill transfer and improve policy learning by serving as valuable tools for data collection. Additionally, end-to-end approaches that use raw sensory input actions may enhance system resilience to perception errors compared to modular designs, as they can learn to adapt and respond more robustly to such errors182. Further improvements in robotic manipulation can be achieved through dynamic adaptation and constraint handling. For instance, enabling robots to make multistep decisions, such as re-grasping objects at intermediate points, improves precision in confined spaces. Training

Tackling the ‘curse of rarity’. A core challenge in training safety prediction models lies in the issue of estimating rare events in high-dimensional spaces, often referred to as the curse of rarity. This curse of rarity arises from the infrequent occurrence of safety-critical events wherein most experimental scenarios will proceed without incident, leading to sparse and sometimes noisy safety-related data184. As a result, the ML models may struggle to accurately identify and mitigate potential safety risks owing to insufficient exposure to hazardous scenarios during training. To address this, innovative approaches from other safety-critical sectors can provide valuable insights. An intriguing approach adopted by Feng et al. uses dense deep reinforcement learning to overcome data sparsity in naturalistic driving data185.
By modifying Markov decision processes to eliminate non-safetycritical states, that is, ignore the vast set of situations in which vehicle behaviours are benign, this method enhances the density of information from safety-critical events in the training dataset, enabling more effective learning compared to conventional deep reinforcement learning methods. Furthermore, generative models can simulate diverse synthetic scenarios, enriching training datasets with diverse safety-critical events. This approach, combined with the digital safety database as proposed in the section ‘Cognitive safety’, can potentially help build more robust AI-assisted safety assessment and risk management models for SDLs. There remains much room for future research to explore these techniques further, adapting them to the unique challenges of chemical environments to develop reliable safety prediction frameworks.

Strengthening the SDL safety ecosystem
When implemented effectively, SDLs can empower various workflows and democratize access to cutting-edge hardware, personnel and software systems192. However, this broad range of capabilities comes with many risks to all parties involved, such as damaging laboratory assets, contaminating experiments or even injuring ground personnel and scientists. Hence, a robust ecosystem for the safe design and deployment of SDLs is crucial to realize their capabilities while mitigating these risks fully.
Workforce training. Reports by the American Chemical Society193,194 and the National Research Council of the National Academies of Science195 have emphasized that a strong safety culture and robust safety skills are essential to prevent devastating accidents in traditional laboratories, and we are of the opinion that the same holds true for SDLs. Compared to traditional laboratories, SDLs incorporate a larger number of moving parts, including robots and mobile stations, which introduces a different kind of risk to personnel working in proximity196. Although existing chemical safety protocols including wearing the necessary PPE197, handling chemicals in appropriate restricted environments such as fume hoods198, and implementing spill and emergency management199 remain relevant, specialized training is essential for personnel working in human–robot shared spaces. As highlighted in a recently concluded workshop on Accelerated Materials Experimentation Enabled by the Autonomous Materials Innovation Infrastructure (AMII) in support of the 2021 Materials Genome Initiative (MGI) Strategic Plan200, a common gap across materials domains relates to safety and safe use. In addition to the hardware-based safety nets discussed in the previous sections, it is thus crucial to educate personnel about safe maintenance protocols and emergency stopping procedures for different levels of severity in the form of safety-rated monitored stops201. More workforce training is also vital to establish a critical mass of qualified workforce with expertise not only in experimental science, AI and ML, and automation technologies200 but also in the safety practices necessary to operate within these SDL environments. However, we acknowledge that the rarity of safety incidents can lead to complacency over time, making it difficult to sustain the high level of readiness and competency required for effective responses in real life202. Furthermore, as SDLs continue to evolve, the continuous adaptation of safety protocols becomes inevitable, necessitating ongoing safety training and workforce engagement to ensure that personnel are always well-equipped.

Inspired by the biosafety levels used in biology laboratories203, the classification of SDL risk could be structured around a set of four main risk evaluation factors, namely the likelihood of accidents, the severity of accidents, the system complexity, and the level of autonomy of SDL53 (Fig. 4). The likelihood and severity of accidents are directly influenced by the nature of the experimentations (for example, type of chemistries and substances being handled), whereas the level of autonomy takes into account the percentage of human–robot collaborative spaces. SDLs with a higher percentage of collaborative spaces could have a higher risk of human injury, whereas more complex SDLs could face increased risks of system failures, unforeseen interactions or cascading errors. We advocate for active dialogue within the SDL community to collaboratively define a unified risk assessment matrix to facilitate the development of effective safety regulations and protocols. Given the rapid pace of SDL development, this matrix should remain dynamic to incorporate updates based on further technological advancements and emerging risks. We would also like to emphasize that continuous improvements in SDL safety systems, as discussed in previous sections, will also reduce the overall hazard levels over time.
Governing bodies and collaboration between stakeholders. Governing bodies are essential in establishing safe SDLs by facilitating collaboration among various stakeholders such as academics, researchers, industry, safety experts and regulatory agencies. They are also important in defining and enforcing protocols, as well as in certifying safety
standards204. Examples include biosafety protocols by the World Health Organization (WHO), robotic safety standards defined in International Organization for Standardization ISO 15066:2016, and aviation regulations by the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) and International Air Transport Association (IATA), which enhance safety ecosystems within their respective fields by investigating accidents and improving procedures204. Organizations such as the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH), the National Safety Council (NSC), and Campus Safety, Health, and Environmental Management Association (CSHEMA) could further collaborate with the SDL community and safety professionals to create this unified, comprehensive safety framework for chemistry SDLs. Incorporating best practices from industry, such as industrial hygiene assessments, risk management protocols, and hazard evaluation methodologies, would further strengthen this framework. Universities and research institutes could then adapt these guidelines to structure their own safety policies. Additionally, trained safety professionals could have an active role in advising and vetting SDL design and implementation to ensure compliance with these safety regulations. Whereas making the guidelines is one-half of the challenge, enforcing these guidelines is equally important and requires the cooperation of universities, institutions and regulatory authorities. The creation of laboratory safety officer programmes and safety teams is essential in fostering a safety-conscious culture. These teams can conduct regular meetings, provide training and implement safety-focused initiatives205.

Additionally, SDLs could benefit from recording, on-the-edge processing, and retaining critical data for post-incident analysis, similar to ‘black boxes’ or flight recorders in aviation207,208. Instilling trust in autonomous risk assessment and management. The trust in SDLs for autonomous risk assessment and management will hinge on their ability to avoid or resolve both known and unforeseen risks, even in unpredictable scenarios209. Achieving this requires a safety infrastructure that is not only robust but also transparent, continuously improving, and capable of integrating human expertise when necessary. A human-in-the-loop approach offers a balanced strategy, ensuring that autonomous systems can operate independently while leveraging expert oversight in ambiguous or high-stakes situations. The role of human scientists should evolve from direct monitoring to refining and improving the safety mechanisms within SDLs. This shift reduces decision-making latency while maintaining safety and preventing overreliance on the system. To establish greater confidence in autonomous risk assessment, several strategies can be used. First, the development of comprehensive SDL safety evaluation benchmarks can provide a standardized framework to assess the robustness of existing and new safety-critical AI systems. Next, incorporating explainable AI techniques can allow users to validate and understand the reasoning of the system in risk assessment. SDLs can also incorporate active feedback mechanisms and tiered safety checks that escalate concerns when necessary, ensuring human intervention only when required. This prevents both blind trust in automation and excessive manual oversight. Although fully autonomous safety management is an open challenge at present, these approaches can help build a safety framework that meets or exceeds human oversight capabilities, fostering trust without unnecessary dependence on manual intervention.
Towards SDLs for challenging chemistries and high-risk experiments
As chemistry SDLs develop, their scope could expand to include increasingly challenging or high-risk chemistries that would have previously been avoided or approached with great caution. Designing these SDLs demands careful adaptation to specific chemical risks involved. For example, SDLs for highly reactive organometallic chemistries requires stringent inert conditions, as rogue exposure to air and moisture will quench highly sensitive reagents, which can clog tubings and corrode hardware components210. For pyrophoric compounds, such exposures could potentially trigger fires or explosions. Current strategies range from basic system purging or applying an inert gas blanket to advanced approaches such as adapting traditional laboratory apparatus for automation and installing robotic systems within
inert glovebox environments211–213. For instance, Bell et al. recently developed the ‘Schlenkputer’, an autonomous synthesizer that uses a combination of motors, switches and valves, and XDL commands to manage tap positions on a Schlenk line and control vacuum levels, enabling the synthesis of highly air-sensitive and moisture-sensitive compounds213. Although such advances demonstrate the potential of SDLs in handling challenging chemistries, automation in these environments remain nascent. Key research areas include motion planning and spatial optimization in constrained glovebox workspaces214, teleoperation systems for remote operation215, and strategies for long-term robotic maintenance in air-free and moisture-free environments216. Another category of high-risk experimentation involves highly exothermic reactions such as acid–base reactions, organometallic reactions and polymerization reactions that are prone to runaway behaviours, as well as reactions that use shock-sensitive reagents such as peroxides and azides. Improper handling and management can lead to spontaneous pressure and temperature build-ups in reactors, potentially resulting in fires and/or explosions217–219. Strategies should, thus, give priority to mitigating runaway behaviour from both planning and execution aspects. For example, these SDLs can adopt continuous-flow technologies that enhance heat dissipation78,220, as discussed in the section ‘Experiment execution and operation’. Online monitoring, combined with AI-supported dynamic decision-making (as elaborated in the section ‘The role of AI in improving safety’), would also significantly mitigate the risks. Additionally, multi-modal sensing systems would help to detect early warning signs, including violent bubbling, unexpected fume release, abnormal viscosity changes or unusual noises, which can allow SDLs to dynamically adjust reaction conditions or alert personnel in time. Given the stability required in reagent and vessel transfer, these SDLs may also benefit from more precise robotic motion control; a useful direction would be to examine near-misses during robot policy training and development.
Concluding remarks
This is an exciting time for SDLs, as advances in AI and automation rapidly expand the possibilities of autonomous experimentation. These developments must be supported by a comprehensive safety framework that integrates AI-enhanced safeguards and a well-defined safety ecosystem. Although implementing these safety measures requires capital and coordination among stakeholders, many challenges can be mitigated through utilizing existing infrastructure, by expert collaborations and by scaling required safety measures according to an SDL specific hazard profile (as discussed in the section ‘Classification of SDL safety levels’). Although no major SDL-related accidents have been reported to date, to the best of our knowledge, proactive safety strategies and rigorous protocols are essential to prevent future risks as these systems become more autonomous and widely adopted. We encourage the SDL community to consider the following immediate actions, which can provide a solid foundation for safe, reliable and sustainable advancements in SDL technology: 1. Encourage active and open data sharing on near-miss cases and safety measures, facilitated through open-sharing platforms such as Discord forums or disseminated via formal documentation and technical reports, to foster collaborative safety practices and accelerate knowledge sharing. This mirrors early efforts in the DARPA Self-Driving Car Challenge, wherein participants shared technical insights and lessons learned221.

From a broader perspective, regulatory and ethical concerns must also be considered as the SDL field grows and evolves. The potential for SDLs to operate with minimal human oversight raises potential accountability issues, particularly in cases wherein an experiment leads to harmful outcomes.
